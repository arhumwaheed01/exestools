# Fair ways to call on students

By Arhum Waheed · Published 2026-10-20 · Updated 2026-10-20

Hands-up questioning has a well-known problem: the same five students answer everything, and the rest of the class learns that they can switch off. Calling on students at random fixes the "same five" problem, but done badly it swaps one unfairness for another, with a student frozen in front of the class for a question they never had time to think about.

This guide is about doing it well. It draws on classroom research and on how the [classroom spinner](/classroom-spinner) on ExesTools is built, and it is honest about where a random picker helps and where it doesn't.

## What the research says, briefly

Three findings are worth knowing before you change your routine.

**Wait time matters more than who you pick.** Mary Budd Rowe's studies of science classrooms in the 1970s found that teachers typically waited about one second after asking a question. When they waited three seconds or more, students gave longer answers, more students volunteered, and fewer said "I don't know." A random pick with no think time is the worst of both worlds.

**Regular cold calling can increase voluntary participation.** Dallimore, Hertenstein and Platt (*Journal of Management Education*, 2013) studied university classes and found that in classes with frequent cold calling, more students answered questions voluntarily, and students' comfort with participating grew over the term. The key word is *frequent*: when it is routine and expected, it stops feeling like an ambush.

**"No hands up" works when the answer is everyone's job.** Dylan Wiliam's work on formative assessment popularised picking students at random (often with names on lolly sticks) so that every student has to prepare an answer. His point is about thinking, not about catching people out.

None of this says random calling suits every lesson. A discussion where students build on each other's ideas needs a different structure. Random calling works best for checking understanding: short questions, quick answers, many students.

## Why a visible random pick helps

A teacher picking from memory, however fair they try to be, tends to drift toward the students who answer well, sit at the front or make eye contact. Students notice. A pick they can see happen on the board answers the "why always me?" complaint before anyone makes it.

## A no-repeat routine you can run every lesson

Here is the routine I would recommend for a class of 25 to 35. It takes about two minutes to set up once and nothing after that.

### Before the lesson

1. Paste your class list into **Your roster** on the classroom spinner, one name per line. Use first names, or first name and initial if two students share a name ("Maya R." and "Maya T."), because identical lines count as duplicates and only one is kept.
2. Press **Copy** before your first spin. Your full roster is now on your clipboard, so you can restore it after a round.
3. Check that **Remove winner after spin (no repeats)** is on. It is on by default on this page.

### During the lesson

1. **Ask the question first, then spin.** If you spin first, 29 students relax. Ask, then say "thirty seconds to think, then I'll spin."
2. **Give real think time.** Ten to thirty seconds for a recall question, longer for "explain why." Count it on the clock if you need to; most of us underestimate.
3. **Press SPIN** (or the Space key). The picked student answers. Because remove-winner is on, they leave the wheel.
4. **Keep going until the round ends.** Everyone answers once before anyone answers twice. When one name is left, the wheel can't spin (it needs two slices), and that student goes last.
5. **Start a new round** by pasting the roster back in from your clipboard.

### Why the no-repeat rule matters, in numbers

Without remove-winner, a 30-name wheel picks with replacement. The chance that a particular student is *never* picked in 30 spins is (29/30)^30, about 36%. So in a typical class, around 11 students would go a whole "round" without being asked anything, while others are asked two or three times. That is fair in a strict mathematical sense, but it doesn't feel fair to the student called three times, and it isn't useful for checking understanding across the room. Removing winners guarantees exactly one turn each per round.

## Making it kind as well as fair

Random doesn't mean relentless. These adjustments keep the routine fair without making it stressful.

- **Warm call with a heads-up.** Tell the class, "After the pair discussion I'll spin for two people to share what their partner said." Students who share a partner's idea are less exposed than students giving their own.
- **Think-pair-share first.** Pairs talk for a minute before the spin. The picked student is reporting a conversation, not performing alone. For quick partners, the [random team generator](/random-team-generator) has a Pairs option; with an odd number of students, one pair becomes a group of three so nobody is left out.
- **Allow "pass and come back."** A student can say "Can you come back to me?" You spin again, and return to them after one or two answers. They still answer, but with more time.
- **Accept partial answers.** "I got as far as the second step" is a real answer. Follow up with the next name: "Can you take it from there?"
- **Speak privately to students with documented needs.** Some students have plans that limit cold calling. Respect those first. You can leave a name off the wheel and talk to that student another way; the routine doesn't need every name on it to be fair to the rest.

## Absences, late arrivals and mid-lesson changes

**A student is absent.** Delete their line before you spin. Spinning and then skipping them when they come up wouldn't change anyone's odds, but it wastes a spin, breaks the rhythm, and announces to the room who is missing. Removing the line keeps every spin useful and every present student on equal terms.

**A student arrives late.** Add their name as a new line. They join the current round.

**You need to stop halfway.** Your list is saved in this browser on this device, so the remaining names are still there next lesson if you use the same computer and browser. If you want a full round next time instead, paste the roster back in.

## Frequently asked questions

### Doesn't cold calling make anxious students more anxious?

It can, if it is rare and comes without warning. Research on frequent cold calling suggests comfort grows when it is routine. Give think time, use think-pair-share, allow "pass and come back," and follow any individual plans first.

### How long should I wait after asking a question?

At least three seconds, and usually longer. Mary Budd Rowe's research found answers improved noticeably once teachers waited three seconds or more. For harder questions, 20 to 30 seconds of think time is reasonable.

### What happens when only one name is left on the wheel?

The wheel needs at least two slices to spin, so that student goes last. Paste your full roster back in to start a new round.

### Should I spin before or after asking the question?

After. If students know who is answering before the question, everyone else stops thinking. Ask, wait, then spin.

### Can a co-teacher use the same list?

Yes. Press Copy share link and send it to them. The names are inside the link, so follow your school's policy on sharing student names.
