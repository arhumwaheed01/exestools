# 10 classroom games to play with a spinner wheel

By Arhum Waheed · Published 2026-10-23 · Updated 2026-10-23

A spinner on the board does something a worksheet can't: it makes the next thirty seconds unpredictable. Kids lean in when they don't know what's coming. That's the whole trick, and it works from kindergarten up to about eighth grade. After that you need to be a little more ironic about it.

Every game below runs on the [classroom spinner](/classroom-spinner) or the [random number wheel](/random-number-wheel). No account, no student logins, and the list you type stays in your browser. Each one has the setup, how it plays, and roughly how long it takes. Pick two or three you like and ignore the rest.

## Review games

### 1. Spin the question type

**Setup:** Replace the roster with question types, not names: "Define it," "Use it in a sentence," "Give an example," "Draw it," "Opposite," "Explain to a partner."
**Play:** Write this week's vocabulary on the board. Point at a word, spin, and the whole class answers in the style the wheel landed on. Mini-whiteboards work well here.
**Time:** 5–10 minutes.

This is my favorite of the lot because it stretches one word list into six different kinds of thinking. "Use *erosion* in a sentence" is a different task from "draw *erosion*," and the kids who struggle with one often shine at the other.

### 2. Points wheel quiz

**Setup:** A wheel of point values: 1, 2, 3, 5, 10, and one "Double" slice. Split the class into two or three teams.
**Play:** Ask a question. The team that answers correctly spins for its points.
**Time:** As long as you've got questions.

Equal slices mean 10 points is exactly as likely as 1. Kids will swear the wheel "hates" their team. It doesn't. That's a nice two-minute probability chat if you want one.

### 3. Who answers, who checks

**Setup:** Your roster, with remove-winner on (it's on by default on the classroom spinner).
**Play:** Ask, give think time, spin for the answerer. Then spin again for a "checker" who says whether they agree and why.
**Time:** Fits any review.

The checker is the important part. It turns one student's answer into a second student's thinking, and it means a wrong answer becomes something to discuss instead of a dead end.

## Warm-ups

### 4. Number of the day

**Setup:** Random number wheel, range 1 to 100 (or 1 to 20 for younger kids).
**Play:** Spin once. Everyone writes as many facts about that number as they can in two minutes: odd or even, ten more, ten less, its factors, how to write it in words, a picture of it in tens and ones.
**Time:** 5 minutes.

Collect the best facts on the board. Strong mathematicians go deep (prime factorization), and early learners still have lots to write. Everybody's working on the same number.

### 5. Letter race

**Setup:** Load **Letters A–Z** on the classroom spinner. Keep remove-winner on so letters don't repeat.
**Play:** Pick a category ("animals," "countries," "things in a kitchen"). Spin a letter. Pairs have 30 seconds to write as many as they can.
**Time:** 5 minutes for four or five rounds.

Twenty-six letters means some rounds land on X or Q. Let it happen once; it's funny. If it keeps happening, delete the hard letters from the list before you start.

### 6. Two-spin math

**Setup:** Random number wheel, range 1 to 12.
**Play:** Spin twice. Write both numbers on the board. The class adds, subtracts, multiplies, or makes the biggest two-digit number they can from the digits.
**Time:** 3–5 minutes.

Leave No repeats off here. Getting 7 twice is a legitimate math problem, and a nice moment for "is that allowed?"

## Brain breaks and movement

### 7. Brain break wheel

**Setup:** Load **Brain break activities** and edit it to what your room can handle. Ten star jumps, a stretch, "touch something blue," a 20-second dance, a silent high-five with a neighbor.
**Play:** Spin when energy drops. Do the thing. Back to work.
**Time:** 1–2 minutes.

Take anything off the list that doesn't suit your space or your students. A brain break that half the class can't join isn't a break.

### 8. Freeze and spin

**Setup:** A wheel of short movement prompts.
**Play:** Students move around while music plays. Stop the music, everyone freezes, spin, and the class does what the wheel says before the music starts again.
**Time:** 5 minutes. Great before a test or after lunch.

## End-of-day fillers

### 9. Mystery job

**Setup:** Load **Classroom jobs** and edit it: chairs up, library books back, whiteboards wiped, line leader for dismissal.
**Play:** Spin the roster for each job. With remove-winner on, nobody gets two jobs in one afternoon.
**Time:** 2 minutes, and the room's tidy.

Don't add "clean the sink" as a punishment slot. Once a spinner hands out consequences, kids start dreading it, and you lose all the good uses above.

### 10. Tell me one thing

**Setup:** Your roster.
**Play:** Five minutes before the bell, spin three or four names. Each student says one thing they learned today, or one thing they're still confused about.
**Time:** 5 minutes.

"Still confused about" is the more useful answer for you. Thank whoever says it, sincerely. You've just found tomorrow's starter.

## A worked example: one 12-minute review block

Say it's Thursday and you have a science quiz tomorrow on the water cycle. Here's how three of these games fit together:

1. **Minutes 0–2:** Load Brain break activities, spin once, do it. Everyone's awake.
2. **Minutes 2–8:** Spin the question type (game 1) over five words: *evaporation, condensation, precipitation, runoff, collection.* That's five spins, one word each. Whiteboards up after every round.
3. **Minutes 8–12:** Who answers, who checks (game 3). Four questions, eight students, nobody repeats because remove-winner is on.

That's 12 minutes, three wheels, zero worksheets. If you want to keep these setups for next time, press **Copy share link** for each wheel and save the links as bookmarks. Opening a bookmark loads that exact wheel again.

## A few things that make spinner games work

**Show the wheel before you spin it.** Kids trust what they can see. Scroll the list once so everyone knows what's on it.

**Read the result out loud.** The back row can't always see a small label on a projector.

**Let a student press SPIN now and then.** It shows the class you're not steering the result.

**Keep the slices short.** "Draw it" fits on a slice. "Draw a diagram that explains the word" does not. The wheel holds up to 60 entries, but six to twelve slices is easier to read from a desk.

**Have a pass rule.** For anything where one student is in the spotlight, decide ahead of time whether they can say "come back to me." For more on calling on students without anxiety, see [fair ways to call on students](/guides/cold-calling-students-fairly).

## Frequently asked questions

### What's the best spinner game for a class that's just learning to read?

Letter race (game 5) with a small set of letters you've taught, or a sight-word version of game 1 where the slices are "Say it," "Spell it" and "Find it in the book." Keep rounds short.

### Can I save different wheels for different games?

The page keeps one list in your browser at a time. To keep several, press Copy share link on each wheel and bookmark the links with clear names like "Question types" or "Brain breaks."

### How do I stop the same student getting picked over and over?

Leave remove-winner on. Each student leaves the wheel after they're picked, so everyone gets a turn before anyone goes twice. Paste the full roster back in to start a new round.

### Is it safe to put students' names in the spinner?

The list is saved only in your browser on that device. Names leave the device only if you share a link, because the link carries the list. Follow your school's policy, and use first names or initials if you prefer.

### Do these games work for older students?

Most do. Games 1, 3 and 6 work well up through middle school. Older teens tend to prefer a spinner used for questions and turn order rather than as the main event.
