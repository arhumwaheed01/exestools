## When a random answer is actually the better way to decide

Most small decisions don't deserve the time we give them. If both options are fine and you've already spent longer weighing them than the choice is worth, the useful question stops being "which is better?" and becomes "how do I stop deciding?" That is the job a 50/50 spin does well.

There is also some evidence that being stuck is itself a bias. The economist Steven Levitt asked people who couldn't make up their minds about a change in their lives, from small things like starting an exercise routine to bigger ones like leaving a job, to flip a coin online, with heads meaning "make the change." About 20,000 tosses were recorded. People who got heads were noticeably more likely to make the change, and when they were surveyed two and six months later, those who had made a change reported being happier on average than those who hadn't (*Review of Economic Studies*, 2021). Levitt's reading was that people on the fence tend to lean too hard toward keeping things as they are.

That study has limits worth taking seriously. The people who took part chose to be there, they were already undecided, and "happier on average" says nothing about any one person. It isn't a reason to let a wheel run your life, and this page still isn't the place for health, money or legal decisions.

What it does suggest is a sensible way to use the wheel:

- **Use it when you're genuinely split.** If you already lean one way, you don't need a spin; you need to act on the lean.
- **Make Yes the change.** Phrase the question so Yes means doing the new thing. If inertia is the bias, that is the side that needs the push.
- **Agree to follow the result, for small things.** A spin only ends the deliberation if you commit to it before you press SPIN.
- **For bigger things, treat the result as a prompt.** Spin, then ask whether you're relieved or disappointed. Take that reaction, not the slice, into the real decision, and give it the time it needs.
